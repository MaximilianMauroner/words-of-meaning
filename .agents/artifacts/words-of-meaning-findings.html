Words of Meaning: an adjective only moves the output when it points away from the default

A blind identification benchmark over 210 isolated model runs. A generator writes a poem, a paragraph, or a React panel under one injected adjective. A separate blind judge, which never sees the prompt, tries to name the adjective from the output alone. Across 179 eligible writing runs the judge was right 25.1% of the time against 16.7% chance. Almost all of that signal came from one family of words.

The finding in one line. Positive aesthetic adjectives such as beautiful and lovely are close to no-ops, because the model already writes at the top of the pleasantness scale without being asked. Neutral adjectives such as ordinary and plain leave no measurable trace at all. Only negative adjectives move the output, and they move it far.

What the benchmark does

Each experiment fixes one task and six conditions: five adjectives plus a required no-adjective control. Each condition runs five times. Every run is a three-stage pipeline, and every stage is a separate short-lived process with its own thread and its own temporary workspace.

  1. Generate. A fresh model process receives the task with one adjective spliced into it, and writes output.md.
  2. Classify. A second fresh process receives only the adjective-free task and the generated text, plus a randomized list of the six candidate labels. It returns a probability for each. It never sees the condition, the run index, the generator thread, or the true label.
  3. Profile. A third fresh process scores the same text on a fixed 1 to 7 rubric, also blind.

Truth is joined only in the parent process, at scoring time. A run whose output contains its own condition word is excluded from every accuracy metric, because that is leakage rather than style.

Result 2: the judge can always tell when a negative word was used, and almost never when a neutral one was

Identification is a hard six-way task. A softer question separates the families much more cleanly: did any adjective appear in the prompt at all? That is the probability mass the judge put on every label except control. It is a single number per run, and it does not require the judge to pick the right synonym.

Three regimes fall out of that table.

  • Negative words are fully legible. Every one of the 49 eligible negative runs was flagged as worded, and every one of the 10 controls was flagged as clean. That is 59 of 59 correct on presence.
  • Neutral words are invisible. Only 3 of 50 runs under ordinary, plain, normal, typical, or standard looked worded to the judge. Asking for an ordinary poem produced text the judge could not distinguish from asking for a poem.
  • Positive words are detected, but so are the controls. The mean for positive runs is 0.79, which is high. The problem is that the mean for their own controls is 0.62, and only 3 of 10 controls were recognized as unworded. The unprompted default already looks like someone asked for something beautiful.

Why the pooled number understates the effect. Presence accuracy for the negative family is 100%. The six-way identification score for the same runs is 39.0%. The judge knows something happened long before it can name which synonym caused it. Identification accuracy measures naming, not detection, and the two come apart.

Result 3: predictions collapse onto a prototype word

Within a family the judge does not spread its guesses. It reaches for one prototype and stays there.

Under negative prompts, ugly absorbed 26 of 59 predictions while unsightly was never chosen. Under positive prompts, beautiful and gorgeous took 41 of 60, and pretty and attractive were never chosen. Under neutral prompts, control took 59 of 60. Five distinct neutral adjectives collapsed into "no adjective was used".

This is what caps identification accuracy in the negative family. The effect on the text is unmistakable, but the five negative words produce nearly the same text as each other, so the judge cannot split them.

Result 4: the behavioral scores show where the movement is

A third blind pass scores each output on seven dimensions from 1 to 7, with no knowledge of the condition. Comparing each word against the control of its own experiment turns the classification result into a magnitude.

The asymmetry has a simple cause: a ceiling. Without any adjective at all, control poems already average 6.5 of 7 on pleasantness and control paragraphs average 6.9 of 7. There is almost no headroom above the default. There is a great deal of room below it, and the negative words use all of it.

Reading down the negative table, the shift is not only in valence. Negative tone rises by 4.1 to 5.7 points, conventionality drops by 1.2 to 2.0 points, and formality drops as well, so a negative adjective also buys rougher and less predictable diction. Sensory imagery barely moves in any family, which suggests the model treats density of detail as part of the task rather than part of the adjective.

The neutral table is the flattest thing in the study. Across five words and seven dimensions its largest single shift is −0.6 points, on pleasantness under ordinary. For comparison, the largest shift in the negative family is +5.7 points. Asking for ordinary output makes the output very slightly more expected, and nothing else.

The code experiment, for contrast

The earlier experiment used the same machinery on a React settings panel with the words lovely, beautiful, elegant, polished, and refined. It scored 5 of 30, or 16.7%, which is exactly chance (p = 0.576).

That is consistent with the writing result rather than in tension with it. All five of those words are positive, and the positive family is the family with no headroom. The judge did detect that something was asked for, with a mean presence probability of 0.78 across the five words, but the control sat at 0.72 as well. Behavioral scoring shows the same compressed range: visual ornamentation moves by at most 0.8 points on a 1 to 7 scale.

Each call also received a temporary CODEX_HOME containing only a mode 0600 copy of the credentials, workspace-write sandboxing, no approvals, and no extra readable directories. Judge inputs carry the adjective-free task, one canonicalized source bundle with path-neutral filenames, and either the shuffled candidate list or the fixed rubric. Limits

  • Five runs per condition. One classification is worth 20 percentage points of per-word recall. The family-level and pooled numbers are the ones to trust; individual per-word recalls are noisy.
  • One judge, one model. Generator and judge are the same model at the same reasoning level. The judge's collapse onto ugly and beautiful may be a property of that judge rather than of the text. A second judge from a different family would separate those explanations.
  • Behavioral, not internal. This measures whether an instruction leaves a recognizable trace in the output. It says nothing about what the model represents internally, and nothing about whether it has a definition of any of these words.
  • Two tasks. A twelve-line autumn poem and a bakery paragraph. Both are warm, domestic, and pleasant by default, which is exactly the condition that creates the positive-side ceiling. A task with a cold or neutral default would test the ceiling explanation directly.
  • Presence at a 0.5 threshold. The presence numbers use a fixed cut on a continuous probability. The means in the same table do not depend on that choice, and they tell the same story.
  • Shared provider state. Process, thread, and workspace isolation is verified. Account, model version, and provider-side state are shared across every run and cannot be inspected from here.

What would change the conclusion

  1. Run the same six conditions at 20 to 50 runs each. That is the threshold at which per-word comparison becomes meaningful, rather than family-level comparison.
  2. Mix families inside one candidate list. A six-way choice among beautiful, ordinary, ugly, and control tests whether the judge can place a text on the valence axis when the axis is the thing being asked about.
  3. Run a task whose default is not already pleasant, such as an incident postmortem or an error message. If the ceiling explanation is right, positive adjectives should become detectable there.
  4. Swap the judge model. If ugly stops being an attractor, the collapse is a judge artifact.
